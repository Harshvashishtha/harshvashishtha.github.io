Research

How nervous systems build reliable percepts out of unreliable signals — and, earlier, how single cells hold a physical trait steady across generations.

Current projects

Sequenced cue integration in approach detection

Brains are thought to detect approaching objects by their expansion on the retina. But a real approach produces more than expansion, and expansion is the cue that arrives last. We found that luminance change alone, with no size change at all, creates a convincing sense of approach in both humans and flies, and we identified the fly neurons that drive escape from it. Those neurons combine luminance and expansion synergistically, but only when luminance comes first, matching the order the cues arrive in nature. We call it sequenced cue integration.

Vashistha, Matos, Wu & Clark (2026)

Motion computation in olfaction

Animals from insects to mammals navigate odour landscapes with remarkable precision — finding food, avoiding predators, locating mates. The standard account is that they infer direction indirectly, chiefly from wind. We asked whether the olfactory system instead computes odour motion directly, and found direction-selective responses to moving odour signals that do not depend on airflow.

Santana, Vashistha, Emonet & Clark (2026) Kadakia et al. (2022)

Luminance-induced expansion aftereffect

Adaptation to visual motion produces robust aftereffects: after prolonged viewing of a moving stimulus, a stationary pattern appears to drift the other way — the motion aftereffect. Whether a purely temporal luminance change, with no spatial displacement at all, is sufficient to produce one had been much less explored.

We found that adapting to decreasing sawtooth luminance ramps produces not only the known brightening aftereffect but also an illusory expansion on a subsequently presented uniform grey screen. No spatial gradient is required for the percept. We call it the luminance-induced expansion aftereffect, or Lexa.

Vashistha & Clark (2026), in press at Perception

Past projects

Cell size regulation in E. coli

Cell size regulation is fundamental to homeostasis, and depends on nutrients, environment and genetics together. E. coli uses dedicated molecular machinery to monitor its own size and coordinate growth with division. We built a phenomenological model of size homeostasis and then perturbed the molecular components implicated in size monitoring — altering the relative expression of the Min proteins — to test their contribution experimentally.

Vashistha et al. (2023) Kohram et al. (2021) Susman et al. (2018)

Non-genetic inheritance and phenotypic variability

Stochastic biochemistry makes isogenic cells differ from one another over time. That heterogeneity helps a population survive changing environments, but it is partly restrained by what cells inherit from their parents. By measuring the inheritance dynamics of sister-cell properties, we asked how fast cellular properties drift and how reliable non-genetic inheritance actually is.

Non-genetic inheritance turns out to shape cell properties over several generations, rather than the one or two generations previously assumed — a result corroborated by a theoretical model that reproduces multigenerational memory between sister cells.

Vashistha, Kohram & Salman (2021) Stawsky et al. (2022) ElGamel et al. (2023)
